# Before Your Clinic Adopts Healthcare AI, Ask These Five Questions

Healthcare technology in India is entering a more practical phase.

On October 14, the National Medical Commission issued an advisory on digitally authenticating medical records. It emphasised that doctors must review relevant clinical information before signing records and that digital authentication does not remove their professional responsibilities.

Around the same time, discussions at a clinical AI summit in Mumbai focused on a related issue: healthcare technology must be safe, appropriately validated, and useful in real clinical settings.

These developments point to a question every clinic owner should consider.

**Does this technology actually help your clinic deliver care, or does it simply add another screen to the workflow?**

## The real test of healthcare technology happens inside the clinic

A product demonstration can look impressive. A dashboard can display detailed information. An algorithm can generate a score in seconds.

But a busy outpatient clinic has different demands.

Patients arrive with different complaints. Staff collect information while managing registration and appointments. Doctors must understand the available information and make decisions based on their clinical judgement.

If a digital tool adds unnecessary steps, produces unclear outputs, or disrupts existing work, its technical capabilities may not translate into practical value.

That is why choosing healthcare technology requires more than comparing features.

## Five questions to ask before adopting healthcare AI

### 1. Does it solve a problem we actually have?

Start with your clinic's daily operations.

Are staff repeatedly collecting the same information? Do doctors enter consultations without basic patient details? Is the reception team struggling to organise incoming patients?

Identify the specific problem before evaluating the software.

A useful tool should address a real operational need rather than create a new process simply because the technology makes it possible.

### 2. Can patients and staff use it easily?

Technology only works when people can use it consistently.

Consider whether patients need to download an application, whether the questions are easy to understand, and whether staff can support patients who need assistance.

Also consider how the tool fits into your existing registration and consultation process.

A complicated system can create additional work even when its underlying technology is sophisticated.

### 3. Can the doctor understand and question its output?

A score or recommendation should never become a substitute for professional judgement.

Doctors need to understand what information a tool uses, what its output means, and when they should disregard it.

For any clinical prioritisation system, ask how it handles incomplete information, unusual presentations, and situations that require immediate human assessment.

The doctor must remain in control of clinical decisions.

### 4. What evidence supports its claims?

Ask vendors to distinguish between what the product can do in a demonstration and what has been established in actual clinical use.

If a company claims to reduce waiting times, improve efficiency, or identify urgent cases, ask how those outcomes were measured.

Was the product evaluated in a real clinic? How many patients were included? What were the limitations? Were errors and exceptions examined?

A promising prototype is not the same as a clinically validated system.

Honest answers to these questions are a sign of a more responsible technology partner.

### 5. How does it protect patient information?

Patient information deserves careful handling, regardless of whether it is stored on paper or digitally.

Ask what information the system collects, who can access it, how it is protected, and how long it is retained.

Understand the arrangements for consent, data sharing, access permissions, and deletion where applicable.

Convenience should not come at the expense of patient confidentiality.

## The next phase of healthcare technology must earn trust

India's digital healthcare progress creates opportunities for new tools, but adoption alone is not the measure of success.

The more important questions are whether a tool addresses a genuine need, fits the way healthcare professionals work, protects patient information, and provides value that can be demonstrated.

For clinic owners, the best technology is not necessarily the product with the longest feature list.

It is the product that solves a clear problem while respecting the judgement of the people responsible for patient care.
